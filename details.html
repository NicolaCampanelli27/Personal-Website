Paper, 2024

Teaching a hybrid car to save fuel

L. Rolando, N. Campanelli, L. Tresca, L. Pulvirenti and F. Millo. "Development of a Soft-Actor Critic Reinforcement Learning Algorithm for the Energy Management of a Hybrid Electric Vehicle". SAE Technical Paper 2024-37-0011, Politecnico di Torino.

The problem

A plug-in hybrid has two ways to move: the engine and the electric motor. At every instant, a controller called the Energy Management System (EMS) decides how to split the work. That choice decides how much fuel the car burns.

Dynamic Programming (DP) finds the mathematically best split, but it needs to know the whole trip in advance and a lot of computing time, so a car cannot use it live. Simple rule-based controllers run in real time, but they are usually far from optimal. Reinforcement learning (RL) offers a third way: an agent learns by trial and error, then runs in real time. The question of this paper: how close to the DP optimum can it get?

The vehicle

The test vehicle is a state-of-the-art diesel plug-in hybrid sold in Europe, with a parallel P2 architecture: the engine and the electric motor both feed a 9-speed automatic gearbox that drives the rear axle.

Training an agent takes hundreds of simulated cycles, so the original digital twin was simplified to a fast backward-kinematic model in Python and validated against experimental data from the real car.

How the agent learns

The agent is a Soft Actor-Critic (SAC). It has two parts: an actor that proposes what to do, and a critic that judges how good that was. Its actor is stochastic: for each situation it outputs a bell curve of possible actions, which keeps it exploring instead of getting stuck. After training, the randomness is switched off and the agent acts deterministically.

Two ways to reward the agent

In RL the reward is everything: a poorly designed one gives poor results no matter how long you train. The goal is minimum fuel while ending the cycle with the same battery charge it started with (charge sustaining). RL cannot enforce that as a hard constraint like DP does, so the reward has to do it.

Case 1: always nudge towards the target

r = k1 − k2·ṁf·Δt − k3·(SoC − SoCtrg)²Penalizes fuel flow and any distance of the battery charge (SoC) from the target.

Case 2: free inside a band

r = k1 − (k2·ṁf + k3·PSoC)·ΔtThe SoC penalty P_SoC is zero while the charge stays between two boundary lines, and grows only outside them.

Case 2 lets the agent use the battery freely as long as it stays in the band. In the last third of the cycle the band narrows, nudging the agent to end at the target charge. For each reward, the weight k₃ was swept over five values (200 to 400 with k₂ fixed at 200), giving the 10 trained agents.

What came out

All ten agents kept the battery close to the target. The best one, Case 2 with k₃ = 300 (called 2C in the paper), balanced a near-perfect final charge with low fuel use. It was then tested against DP on the training cycle (WLTC) and on two cycles it had never seen: the urban FTP75 and the highway HFET.

  • Different strategy, similar result. The agent drains the battery more at the start of the cycle and relies more on the electric motor, then uses the engine to recharge later, while DP keeps the charge steadier.
  • Where it loses. The agent's engine operating points are more scattered on the efficiency map, including inefficient low-load points, while DP stays closer to the optimal operating line.
  • The urban cycle is harder. On FTP75 the fuel use was 17.5% above DP and the charge ended 2% high. The extra charge explains part of it, not all.
Honest reading. The agent never saw FTP75 or HFET during training and stayed within about 17.5% of the optimum on both, without perfect charge sustainability. That is encouraging for a first, untuned version, and it is exactly where the next steps start.

The hyperparameters were kept fixed on purpose, so that any difference in results comes from the reward and not from tuning. A model was saved every 20 episodes.

Tools: a Python environment built to the Gymnasium standard, the SAC implementation from Stable-Baselines3, and an open-source MATLAB Dynamic Programming code from ETH Zurich for the benchmark.

What's next

Tune the hyperparameters of the networks, extend the agent to charge-depleting operation, train it over many driving cycles representing different patterns, and eventually run the approach on a high-fidelity forward-dynamics model of the car.

Is a battery storage worth it?

N. Campanelli and M. D'Arpino. "Optimal control and techno-economic analysis of long-duration battery energy storage system applications". Journal of Energy Storage, vol. 164, 2026. The Ohio State University.

The question

Long-duration batteries can hold energy for 10 hours or more. They promise to cut electricity bills, back up renewables and help the grid, but they are expensive. Is the investment worth it?

The usual tool to compare storage costs is the Levelized Cost of Storage (LCOS): the price at which each stored kWh must be sold to repay the project. Traditional calculators assume the battery cycles at a constant rate with a constant efficiency and a fixed energy price. Real batteries do not behave like that: they charge when power is cheap and discharge when it is expensive or when the building peaks.

The contribution of this work is to put the optimal control of the battery inside the cost analysis, so the LCOS is computed from how the battery would actually be operated.

The system that was simulated

A "behind the meter" plant: a commercial building, a wind turbine and a long-duration battery connected to the grid. The building and the wind are uncontrollable; the battery is the only thing the optimizer decides.

The battery is modelled cell by cell with an equivalent circuit built on experimental measurements, including its internal resistance and the efficiency of the converter, so charging and discharging are not "free".

Dynamic Programming looks at the whole year and finds the cheapest sequence of charge and discharge decisions. Demand charges are then computed on the resulting grid power peaks.

Five scenarios

1A. Arbitrage

The battery supports the building by buying cheap and avoiding expensive hours. Demand charges are not part of the optimization.

1B. Arbitrage + peak shaving

As 1A, but the optimizer also caps the power drawn from the grid to cut demand charges.

1C. Adding wind

As 1B, plus power from a wind turbine under a Power Purchase Agreement.

2. Battery alone

No building: the battery only trades energy with the grid, as a pure arbitrage business.

3. Battery at a wind farm

The battery delays the wind farm's sales to the hours when the price is higher.

What the optimal control does

The optimizer charges when electricity is cheap, discharges when it is expensive, and shaves the building's power peaks. It also plans across days. In the April 2022 example of the paper, it keeps storing energy from day 14 to day 17 so that on day 18 the battery can cover most of the building's demand, avoiding a costly day.

That is the point of long duration: the value is not only in the instant price, but in moving energy through time, and in the demand charge it saves on the way.

Results: the bill goes down

A surprise in 1A. Its demand charges ($25,219) are higher than with no battery ($22,131). To exploit very low prices, the optimizer charges at full power and creates a grid peak higher than the building's own. 1B fixes this by limiting the peak.

The catch: storing energy still costs more

With the operation known hour by hour, the LCOS can be computed properly: $0.23 to $0.26 per kWh for the building scenarios. Multiplying it by the energy the battery handles in a year gives what it costs to store that energy. Comparing that with what the battery earns or saves tells you whether the investment pays back.

Would another chemistry change the answer?

The same optimal control was applied to the cost data of other battery types for Scenario 2. Lithium-ion (LFP) is the cheapest to store with, at $0.1726 per kWh. But for that scenario to break even, the LCOS would need to fall to about $0.1173 per kWh, roughly half of today's value, and the DOE target for 2030 is lower still.

Takeaways

  • Operationally, it works. A long-duration battery can cut a commercial building's bill by up to 20.4%, and by 32.4% together with wind.
  • Economically, not yet. At current cost assumptions the cost of storage exceeds savings and revenues in every scenario.
  • Method matters. Putting optimal control and real data inside the cost analysis changes the answer compared with a lumped calculation, and shows how scenarios differ.
